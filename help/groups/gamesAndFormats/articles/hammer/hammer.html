Match Play

The Hammer

Also known as: Throw the Hammer, Double the Hole

The Hammer is a classic way to turn up the heat in a match-play game. It lets players double the value of a hole while they’re scoring it (and double it again) so a single hole can be worth as much as the rest of the match. In Squabbit, the Hammer is not a separate game. It’s a toggle you layer on top of a hole-by-hole match-play game, giving you a quick way to raise the stakes on the holes that matter.

What the Hammer Is

In a match-play game, each hole is normally worth the same amount: win the hole and you go one up. The Hammer breaks that pattern. By “throwing the hammer” on a hole, a player doubles how much that hole counts toward the match. The reward goes to whoever wins the hole, so a Hammer is a bet on your own game: raise the stakes when you like your chances.

Squabbit sums up the feature in one line: “Doubles this hole’s value in the match. Whoever wins the hole collects the multiplied amount.” You can hammer a hole, then hammer it again to double it a second time, right up to eight times the normal value.

It’s a match-play add-on, not a standalone game. The Hammer only appears on hole-by-hole match-play games: the kind where each hole is won, lost, or halved. It doesn’t apply to total-score games, where there’s no individual hole value to double.

How the Hammer Works
  1. Every hole in the match starts at its normal value: a plain 1× hole.
  2. While scoring a hole, a player in the match can throw the hammer on it, choosing to make it worth 2×, 4×, or 8× the normal hole value.
  3. When the hole is decided, whoever wins it collects the multiplied amount. A 2× hole is worth two normal holes in the match; an 8× hole is worth eight.
  4. A halved (tied) hole is still worth nothing, no matter the multiplier: doubling nothing is still nothing.
  5. You can clear the hammer back to its normal 1× value at any time while that hole’s score screen is open.

When can you hammer?

The Hammer lives on the hole’s score-entry screen. Any player taking part in a hammer-enabled match-play game sees a Hammer row there, with chips for 2×, 4×, and 8×. Traditionally players throw the hammer before a key shot to put the pressure on, but Squabbit simply lets you set the multiplier on the hole while you score it. Tap a chip to set it; tap the selected chip again to clear it.

How the doubling adds up: The chips are the hole’s total value, not a running tally: 2× is one double, 4× is a second double, and 8× is a third. That mirrors the old-school hammer, where each throw doubles the last: 1 → 2 → 4 → 8.

Example Match

Player A and Player B are playing a 1v1 Singles Match Play game with the Hammer turned on:

Hole 4: Normal (1×)
Player A wins the hole with a par.
Player A goes 1 up in the match.
Hole 7: Hammer thrown (2×)
Player B likes a reachable par 5 and hammers the hole to 2×.
Player B wins the hole with a birdie.
The hole counts double, so Player B swings the match by 2, back to all square.
Hole 18: Hammered twice (4×)
With the match on the line, the closing hole is hammered up to 4×.
Player A wins the hole.
The hammered hole is worth 4, so Player A collects four holes’ worth of the match in one swing.
Why it’s exciting: A hammered hole can erase a lead or close out a match in a single swing. Because the multiplier counts toward the running total, a big hammer can even clinch the match early if the resulting lead is more than the remaining holes could ever claw back.

How It Settles

The Hammer doesn’t change how a match is won: it only changes how much each hole counts. Squabbit runs the match exactly as it normally would, with one difference: a hammered hole contributes its multiplied value to the running tally instead of a single hole.

The running match tally

Win a normal hole and you go 1 up. Win a 2× hole and you go 2 up; win an 8× hole and you go 8 up. The side with the higher total at the end wins the match, and the result is shown the usual way: for example a win margin, “all square” if the totals are level, or a closed-out match.

Clinching early

Match play ends as soon as one side’s lead is bigger than the most the remaining holes could swing back. Because a hammered hole adds its full multiplied value to the lead, a big hammer can push a match to that point sooner than a string of normal holes would. There’s no separate “Hammer clinch”: it’s the same early-finish math that already decides every match play game, just reached faster.

Purse and payouts

If your game is playing for a purse, the payout reads the same hole values the match tally does. So “whoever wins the hole collects the multiplied amount” applies to the money as well: winning a hammered hole is worth its full multiplier in the purse.

No accept-or-forfeit step: Some bar-room versions of the Hammer require your opponent to accept the double or concede the hole. Squabbit keeps it simple: the hammer sets the hole’s value and whoever wins the hole on the card collects it.

Setting Up in Squabbit

The Hammer is a single switch you turn on when you’re playing a hole-by-hole match-play game.

  1. Create or edit a match-play game: one where each hole is won, lost, or halved (for example Singles Match Play, Team Match Play, or Low Ball).
  2. Open the game’s settings and flip the Hammer switch on. It’s off by default. The setting reads: “Let players double a hole’s value during the match (and double again) while scoring.”
  3. Add your players and start the round.
  4. During play, open a hole’s score screen. You’ll see a Hammer row with 2×, 4×, and 8× chips.
  5. Tap a chip to hammer the hole, then tap Done. The match tally updates right away to reflect the multiplied hole.

On the scorecard, a hammered hole is marked with a small multiplier badge (such as 2×) in the corner of the cell, so everyone can see at a glance which holes were doubled and by how much.

Note: The Hammer only shows up when your game is scored as hole-by-hole match play. If you don’t see the Hammer switch, check that your game’s scoring is set to a match-play format: total-score games don’t have individual hole values to double.
